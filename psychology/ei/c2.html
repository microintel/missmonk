Emotional Intelligence — Ch. 2

Anatomy of an
Emotional Hijacking

Two signals leave your senses at the same time. One goes to your feeling brain. One goes to your thinking brain. Watch which one always gets there first.

● fast path — feels first ● slow path — understands fully
Signal 01 · Fast path

Strong feelings can take over your brain in a split second.

This is called an emotional hijack. It happens before you even know it's happening.

Transmission log
I just went bananas. My head just exploded.
Signal 02 · Command center

The hijack happens in a tiny brain part called the amygdala.

It's the brain's alarm system. It watches for danger, fear, and passion, all day, every day.

Signal 03 · The race

The amygdala can act before your thinking brain even catches up.

A signal from your eyes or ears reaches the amygdala first. Your neocortex, the thinking part, gets it a beat later.

Signal 04 · The shortcut

There's a wire running straight from your senses to the amygdala.

It skips the thinking brain completely. Scientists call it a neural back alley.

Transmission log
Anatomically the emotional system can act independently of the neocortex.
Signal 05 · Fast but sloppy

This shortcut is quick, but it isn't careful.

It reacts to rough matches, not exact ones. Close enough to an old danger is close enough to set off the alarm.

Transmission log
Some emotional reactions and emotional memories can be formed without any conscious, cognitive participation at all.
Signal 06 · Emotional memory

The amygdala also stores how you felt, not just what happened.

Facts live in a different part of the brain. The amygdala keeps the feeling attached to them.

Transmission log
The hippocampus is crucial in recognizing a face as that of your cousin. But it is the amygdala that adds you don't really like her.
Signal 07 · Burned in deeper

Big emotions burn memories in deeper than ordinary ones.

That's why you can still picture a first date, or a scary moment, years later, in sharp detail.

Signal 08 · Old alarms

These alarms can go off using out-of-date information.

The amygdala can fire an old fear in a brand new moment that only looks a little bit similar.

Main lesson

Your feelings can move faster than your thoughts. That's not a flaw — it's your brain trying to keep you safe, even when it gets the moment wrong.

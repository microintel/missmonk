Chapter Eight

Enlightened
Relationships

On love, attachment, the pain-body, and what it means to stay present with another person.

↓ scroll
01

Don't wait for the future to make you happy

A common way of thinking is:

"When I get a partner, I'll be happy."

"When I get married, everything will be perfect."

"When I get a good job, then I'll feel complete."

The book says this creates a future-based search for happiness.

For example

Someone feels lonely and thinks:

"If I find the right person, my loneliness will disappear."

They find someone and feel extremely happy for some time. But later, fear, jealousy, insecurity, or dissatisfaction may return.

The idea is that happiness cannot permanently come from something outside you. The book calls true "salvation" a state of inner peace and fulfillment that is available in the present moment.

02

Love can become attachment

The book makes an important distinction between love and attachment.

Imagine:

"I love you."

But underneath that feeling is:

"I need you."

"Don't leave me."

"You must always give me attention."

"If you talk to someone else, I'll become jealous."

Now fear of losing the person becomes part of the relationship.

The book describes this as addictive attachment rather than true love.

Simple example

Person A:

"I can't live without you."

At first it sounds romantic.

But psychologically, it can mean:

"My sense of security depends completely on you."

Then if the person doesn't reply for several hours:

Love → fear → overthinking → anger → accusation

That cycle can create suffering.

03

Why relationships can change from love to hate

Have you noticed situations like:

Morning:

"I love you ❤️"

Evening:

"I never want to talk to you again!"

The book says relationships can sometimes move between opposite emotional states:

Love → attachment → fear → jealousy → anger → conflict → reconciliation → love → conflict again.

Some couples even become attached to this emotional drama because intense emotions make them feel alive.

Example

A couple argues.

One says:

"You're ignoring me!"

The other says:

"You're always blaming me!"

Then both become defensive.

Instead of solving the original problem, they attack each other.

04

The ego looks for something outside itself

The book uses the word ego for the mind-created sense of "me."

For example:

"I am successful."

"I am attractive."

"I am someone's boyfriend/girlfriend."

"I am respected."

"I am better than others."

The problem is that if your identity depends on these things, losing them can make you feel that you have lost yourself.

The book says a romantic partner can temporarily become the center of this identity:

"This person gives my life meaning."

But if that person changes or leaves, the insecurity underneath can return.

05

Relationships don't necessarily create your pain

This is one of the chapter's major ideas.

Suppose someone already has insecurity.

Their partner doesn't reply to a message.

The mind says:

"They don't care about me."

Then:

thought → fear → anger → accusation

The person may think:

"My partner caused my suffering."

But the book suggests that the relationship can bring existing pain and insecurity to the surface rather than creating it from nothing.

Simple example

Imagine a person who already fears rejection.

Their friend says:

"I can't meet today."

The event is small.

But their mind interprets it as:

"Nobody wants me."

The event triggered something that was already inside.

06

What does "being present" mean?

This is the main practice of the chapter.

Being present means:

Notice what is happening inside you instead of immediately becoming the reaction.

For example:

Someone insults you.

Normal reaction:

"How dare you!"

Then you immediately attack back.

Presence means:

"I'm feeling anger right now."

Notice:

anger in the body

thoughts appearing

urge to respond

tension

desire to attack

You don't have to pretend the anger isn't there.

You simply observe it before automatically acting on it.

The book describes this as becoming the "silent watcher" of your thoughts and reactions.

07

Don't judge yourself or your partner immediately

Suppose your partner becomes angry.

Instead of:

"You're an horrible person."

Try recognizing:

"Anger is happening right now."

This doesn't mean accepting harmful behavior.

It means seeing the situation clearly without immediately becoming consumed by your own reaction.

The book calls this moving from reaction to knowing.

08

Communicate without attacking

The chapter recommends expressing feelings without blaming.

Instead of:

"You never listen to me!"

Try:

"I'm feeling ignored right now, and I'd like you to listen."

Instead of:

"You always make me angry!"

Try:

"I'm noticing that I'm becoming angry. Can we talk about what happened?"

The difference is:

Blaming:

"You are the problem."

Awareness:

"This is what I'm experiencing right now."

The book also emphasizes listening without immediately becoming defensive.

09

Relationship problems can become opportunities

This is an interesting idea in the chapter.

Normally people think:

"A good relationship should have no problems."

The book presents another perspective:

Problems can reveal unconscious patterns that you normally don't notice.

Example

Someone criticizes you.

Suddenly you notice:

"Why did that sentence make me so angry?"

You discover:

"I have a strong need to always be right."

Now the conflict has shown you something about yourself.

The book describes this as using the relationship as a spiritual practice — using difficult moments to become more conscious.

10

The "pain-body"

The chapter uses the term pain-body to describe accumulated emotional pain that can become strongly activated.

Think of it as an emotional trigger system.

For example:

Someone says something small:

"You made a mistake."

But suddenly you feel enormous anger.

Why such a big reaction?

The book's explanation is that the present event may have activated older emotional pain.

When this happens, instead of thinking:

"I AM angry."

Try:

"Anger is happening in me right now."

That small difference creates some distance between you and the emotion.

11

Don't become your emotions

This is extremely important.

Instead of:

"I am angry."

You can notice:

"Anger is present."

Instead of:

"I am a failure."

Notice:

"My mind is producing the thought that I'm a failure."

Instead of:

"I am anxious."

Notice:

"Anxiety is being experienced right now."

The goal isn't to suppress the emotion.

It is to observe it without automatically becoming it.

12

What should you actually do?

When a strong emotion appears:

Step 1 — Stop

Don't immediately react.

Step 2 — Notice

Ask:

"What am I feeling right now?"

Step 3 — Feel the body

Notice:

tight chest

fast heartbeat

stomach tension

heat

trembling

Step 4 — Watch the thoughts

Notice what the mind is saying.

For example:

"They don't respect me."

"They always do this."

"I have to fight back."

Step 5 — Don't immediately believe every thought

A thought is something appearing in your mind.

You don't have to act on every thought.

Step 6 — Respond consciously

Once the emotional intensity decreases, decide what actually needs to be said or done.

This is the practical meaning of entering the Now according to the chapter.

13

Love doesn't mean tolerating everything

An important practical point:

Presence ≠ allowing abuse.

Being calm doesn't mean:

"Someone can hurt me and I should do nothing."

You can recognize:

"This behavior is harmful."

and still respond without unnecessary hatred, revenge, or uncontrolled reaction.

The book says that relinquishing judgment doesn't mean failing to recognize dysfunctional behavior.

14

Don't depend on another person to complete you

The chapter eventually goes deeper.

It says that if someone feels uncomfortable being alone, they may search for a relationship to escape that discomfort.

But the discomfort can eventually appear inside the relationship too.

Example

Before relationship:

"I'm lonely."

After relationship:

"Why aren't they texting?"

Later:

"Why don't they understand me?"

Later:

"Are they going to leave me?"

The external situation changed, but the internal uneasiness remained.

The whole chapter in one simple idea

The chapter's central message can be understood like this:

Don't use another person as the solution to your inner unhappiness.

Instead:

Notice → Feel → Observe → Become present → Respond consciously.

A relationship can then become less about:

"You must make me happy."

and more about:

"We can be together while both of us remain aware of what is happening inside."

The book ultimately presents love as something deeper than emotional dependency: a state connected with presence and inner awareness.

One easy example to remember

Without presence

Partner doesn't reply →

"They don't care!" →

Anger →

Accusation →

Argument →

More pain.

With presence

Partner doesn't reply →

"I'm feeling anxious." →

Notice the feeling →

Don't immediately react →

Understand what is actually happening →

Communicate calmly.

That's the practical heart of Chapter 8.

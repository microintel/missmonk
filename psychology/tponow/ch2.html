The Power of Now  ·  Chapter 2

Consciousness:
The Way Out of Pain

Something happens. The mind reacts. We believe the reaction is “me.”
The reaction can create more suffering.

scroll ↓
The Main Idea

Notice the process, instead of being run by it.

A lot of psychological suffering becomes stronger when we resist the present moment and become completely identified with our thoughts and emotions.

In very simple terms

Something happens →

the mind reacts →

we believe the reaction is “me” →

the reaction can create more suffering.

The chapter teaches us to notice this process instead of automatically becoming controlled by it.

01

Pain and suffering are not exactly the same

The book makes an important distinction.

Pain

Pain can be part of life.

For example:

Someone you love dies.

You fail an exam.

A relationship ends.

Someone says something hurtful.

You lose a job.

These events can naturally hurt.

Additional suffering

Then the mind may keep adding thoughts:

“Why did this happen to me?”

“My life is ruined.”

“This should never have happened.”

“What if everything goes wrong?”

“I can't handle this.”

The original event is one thing.

The continuous mental reaction can become another layer of suffering.

Tolle argues that much human suffering is created or intensified by this resistance to what is happening now.

— The Power Of Now, Eckhart Tolle

Everyday example

Imagine someone gets a low mark in an exam.

Event:

“I got 45%.”

That's a fact.

Then the mind says:

“I'm stupid.”

“Everyone is better than me.”

“I'll never succeed.”

“My future is finished.”

Now the person isn't dealing only with the exam result. They're also dealing with a whole story created by the mind.

02

What does “accept the Now” mean?

This is one of the most important ideas in the chapter.

Acceptance does NOT mean

“Everything is good.”

It means

“This is what is happening right now. I will first recognize reality instead of mentally fighting the fact that it is happening.”

The book says:

Accept → then act.

— The Power Of Now, Eckhart Tolle

Example

Suppose your train is delayed by two hours.

You have two possibilities.

Reaction A

“This is terrible! Why does this always happen? I hate this!”

The train is still delayed.

But now there is additional frustration.

Reaction B

“The train is delayed. That's the current situation. What can I do now?”

Then you can:

check another train,

call someone,

read,

work,

wait,

change your plan.

Acceptance doesn't prevent action.

It allows action to happen without first wasting energy fighting reality.

03

The past and future can take us away from the present

The book says the mind frequently moves between:

Past → Present → Future

But we actually experience life only in the present moment.

— The Power Of Now, Eckhart Tolle

Example: Past

Someone made an embarrassing mistake five years ago.

Today they remember it:

“Why did I do that?”

“Everyone must think I'm stupid.”

The event is over.

But the mind is bringing the emotional experience into the present.

Example: Future

Someone has an interview tomorrow.

Their mind starts:

“What if I fail?”

“What if they reject me?”

“What if I can't answer anything?”

The interview hasn't happened yet.

The body is sitting in the present, but the mind is experiencing an imagined future.

This is what Tolle calls an “anxiety gap” between the present and the future imagined by the mind.

— The Power Of Now, Eckhart Tolle

04

But isn't thinking about the future useful?

Yes.

This is important.

The book is not saying that planning is useless.

You need the past and future for practical life.

For example:

“I have an exam next month, so I'll study today.”

That's useful planning.

The problem is when planning becomes:

“What if I fail?”

“What if something goes wrong?”

“What will everyone think?”

“What if my entire future is ruined?”

Now the mind is creating suffering around something that hasn't happened.

Tolle's point is to use past and future when necessary, rather than mentally living there all the time.

— The Power Of Now, Eckhart Tolle

05

What is the “pain-body”?

This is one of the major concepts of the chapter.

Tolle uses “pain-body” to describe accumulated emotional pain that can remain psychologically active and get triggered by situations that resemble earlier painful experiences.

— The Power Of Now, Eckhart Tolle

Think of it as a stored emotional pattern.

Example

Imagine someone was frequently criticized as a child.

Years later, their manager says:

“This work needs improvement.”

The sentence itself might be normal.

But the person suddenly feels:

anger

shame

fear

defensiveness

Why such a strong reaction?

According to Tolle's framework, the present situation may have triggered an older emotional pattern.

06

A small comment can trigger a huge reaction

The book says that even an innocent remark can activate an old pain pattern.

— The Power Of Now, Eckhart Tolle

For example:

Friend

“Why are you so quiet today?”

Person

“Leave me alone!”

The friend may not have intended anything harmful.

But something inside the person was already sensitive.

The important question isn't only:

“Why did my friend say that?”

It can also be:

“Why did that comment produce such a strong reaction inside me?”

That question creates awareness.

07

Being angry vs observing anger

This is probably the most practical lesson in the chapter.

Suppose someone insults you.

Your mind immediately produces:

“How dare they!”

Then more thoughts:

“I'll teach them a lesson.”

“They always disrespect me.”

“I should say something back.”

Now you are inside the anger.

Tolle suggests something different:

“Anger is appearing in me right now.”

Notice:

“I am angry”

versus

“I notice anger happening.”

The second statement creates a little distance between you and the emotion.

The book calls this becoming the “watcher” or “witness.”

— The Power Of Now, Eckhart Tolle

08

You don't have to fight the emotion

This is subtle.

Tolle doesn't recommend:

“I must destroy my anger.”

Instead:

Observe it.

Imagine anger is like a wave.

You don't have to:

suppress it,

immediately act on it,

create a story around it,

identify yourself with it.

You can notice:

“My body is tense.”

“My heart is beating faster.”

“Angry thoughts are appearing.”

“I want to react.”

You're observing what is happening.

The book says that trying to fight the pain-body can create more inner conflict; observation and presence are presented as the way to break identification with it.

— The Power Of Now, Eckhart Tolle

09

“Don't think about it” doesn't mean suppress it

This part can easily be misunderstood.

When Tolle says not to let the feeling turn into thinking, he is describing the difference between feeling an emotion and building a long mental story around it.

— The Power Of Now, Eckhart Tolle

Example:

Feeling

“I'm angry.”

Notice the physical experience.

Story

“He always does this.”

“Nobody respects me.”

“People are horrible.”

“I'm going to get revenge.”

“This always happens to me.”

The second process can keep the emotional reaction alive.

So the practice is:

Feel → observe → don't feed the story → remain present.

10

What does “unconscious” mean in this book?

Tolle uses the word unconscious in a particular way.

He doesn't simply mean:

“You are asleep.”

He means becoming completely identified with a mental or emotional pattern, without noticing that it is happening.

— The Power Of Now, Eckhart Tolle

Example

Someone becomes angry.

They don't notice:

“Anger is happening.”

Instead:

“I AM angry. THEY are the problem. I MUST attack them.”

The emotion has taken control.

Being conscious means noticing:

“Anger is happening inside me.”

That small observation changes the relationship with the emotion.

11

Fear: real danger vs psychological fear

The chapter makes another useful distinction.

Real danger

You're walking near a fire.

You understand:

“If I touch that, I'll get burned.”

You move away.

That's practical intelligence.

Psychological fear

Now imagine you're sitting safely at home thinking:

“What if something terrible happens tomorrow?”

Nothing dangerous is happening right now.

But your body can still react with:

tension,

anxiety,

nervousness,

worry.

Tolle describes this kind of fear as being primarily about something that might happen, rather than something happening now.

— The Power Of Now, Eckhart Tolle

12

Why does arguing become so intense?

This is another interesting idea.

Imagine two people arguing.

Person A

“I'm right!”

Person B

“No, I'm right!”

Now suppose Person A discovers they were wrong.

Why can admitting it feel so uncomfortable?

Because sometimes a person isn't defending merely an idea.

They're defending their identity.

Their mind says:

“If I'm wrong, I'm less valuable.”

So a simple disagreement becomes a threat to the person's sense of self.

Tolle argues that when someone is less identified with their mental positions, they can disagree without needing to aggressively prove themselves right.

— The Power Of Now, Eckhart Tolle

13

Example: two people disagreeing

Person A

“I think this movie is excellent.”

Person B

“I don't like it.”

A mind-identified reaction might be:

“What? How can you be so stupid?”

A more conscious response:

“Interesting. We have different opinions.”

You can still explain your opinion strongly.

But you don't need to turn:

“I disagree with you”

into:

“You are a bad person.”

That's the distinction Tolle is making.

14

The ego's search for “more”

Near the end of the chapter, Tolle discusses what he calls the ego's search for wholeness.

The basic idea is:

“Something is missing in me, so I need something outside myself to make me complete.”

Then the mind may chase:

money,

success,

possessions,

popularity,

recognition,

relationships,

status,

knowledge,

appearance.

The book argues that these things can provide temporary satisfaction, but they don't permanently solve the deeper feeling of incompleteness.

— The Power Of Now, Eckhart Tolle

Example

Someone thinks:

“If I get a better phone, I'll be happy.”

They buy it.

For a while:

“This is amazing!”

After some time:

“Maybe I need an even better phone.”

Then the cycle starts again.

The same pattern can happen with:

money → success → followers → relationships → status → possessions.

The object changes, but the psychological search can remain.

15

The entire chapter in one simple story

Imagine a person named Ravi.

Ravi's manager criticizes his work.

Step 1 — Event

Manager

“This report needs improvement.”

Step 2 — Mind reacts

Ravi thinks:

“He doesn't respect me.”

Step 3 — Old pain gets triggered

He remembers previous experiences of being criticized.

Step 4 — Emotion grows

He becomes angry.

Step 5 — More thinking

“Everyone treats me badly.”

Step 6 — Reaction

He sends an angry message.

Step 7 — More problems

The manager becomes angry.

Now Ravi thinks:

“See! Everyone is against me.”

The cycle continues.

Now imagine Ravi becoming aware.

Manager

“This report needs improvement.”

Ravi notices:

“I'm feeling anger.”

He doesn't immediately act.

He notices:

“My body is tense.”

“I'm having defensive thoughts.”

He allows the feeling to be there.

Then he asks:

“What actually needs to be fixed in the report?”

Now he can respond to the actual situation, rather than reacting entirely from the emotional pattern.

That is very close to the practical application of the chapter.

16

The practice Tolle is teaching

You can reduce the whole chapter to this simple sequence:

1. Something happens

↓

2. Notice your reaction

↓

3. Don't immediately become the reaction

↓

4. Observe the feeling

↓

5. Notice the thoughts without automatically believing them

↓

6. Accept that this is what is happening right now

↓

7. Then decide what action is appropriate

This is what the book means by presence and becoming the watcher.

A very simple daily example

Someone sends you a message:

“We need to talk.”

Immediately your mind says:

“Oh no. What happened?”

Then:

“Maybe they're angry.”

Then:

“Maybe I did something wrong.”

Then:

“What if something terrible happened?”

Notice what happened.

The message was only five words.

The mind created an entire future.

A present-focused response would be:

“I don't know what they mean yet.”

Then continue with what is actually happening now.

When the person calls, you'll have the real information.

The Deepest Lesson

Tolle is essentially asking the reader to learn this difference:

❌ “I am my thoughts and emotions.”

versus

✅ “I can observe my thoughts and emotions.”

If you can observe anger, you are not completely lost inside the anger.

If you can observe fear, you are not completely lost inside the fear.

If you can observe worrying thoughts, you are not completely lost inside those thoughts.

That observing awareness is what Tolle calls presence/consciousness.

Notice → allow → stay present → then act.

The Divided Self · Chapter 1

Left vs. Right

Your brain has two halves. One of them talks. And it will make up an answer even when it doesn't know the truth.

each side sees something different — only one side can speak

Idea 1

A doctor cut the wire between the two halves

Real life: Imagine a bad electrical fault in one room of a house, that keeps spreading through the wiring and knocking out power everywhere. Now imagine cutting one wire to stop that spread.

Your brain has a left half and a right half, joined by a thick wire of nerves. In the 1960s, some patients had seizures so bad that a small electrical storm in one half would spread to the other half, and they'd collapse.

A surgeon named Joe Bogen tried something bold: cut that wire. It sounds risky, since that wire is the biggest one in the whole body. But it worked — seizures dropped a lot, and the patients seemed totally fine afterward.

Idea 2

One half talks. The other half can't.

Real life: Your left brain controls your right hand and handles talking. Your right brain controls your left hand and handles things like faces and patterns — but it has no words.

Normally the two halves share everything instantly through that wire. But once the wire is cut, scientists could show something to just one half — say, flash a picture only to the right brain. The person could point at it with their left hand. But if you asked "what did you see?", they'd say "nothing" — because the half that talks never saw it.

Idea 3

A made-up answer, said with full confidence

Real life: A scientist showed one patient two pictures at once — a chicken's claw for his talking (left) brain, and a snowy scene for his silent (right) brain. Then he asked the patient to point at pictures that matched what he saw. The patient's right hand pointed at a chicken. His left hand pointed at a snow shovel.

Now here's the strange part. The scientist asked: "Why did you point at the shovel?" The patient's talking brain never saw the snow — it only knew about the chicken. But instead of saying "I don't know," it instantly answered:

"The chicken claw goes with the chicken, and you need a shovel to clean out the chicken shed."

That's a made-up reason. It sounds totally reasonable — but it has nothing to do with the real cause, which was the snow picture his talking brain never even saw.

Idea 4

Your brain always has an answer ready

Real life: A scientist showed the word "walk" only to a patient's silent (right) brain. The patient stood up and started walking. When asked "why are you getting up?", he said: "I'm going to get a Coke."

He wasn't lying. His talking brain genuinely had no idea the word "walk" caused it. So it did what it always does — it invented a reasonable-sounding answer, and said it with full confidence.

Scientists have a name for this: confabulation. It means making up a believable reason for something you did, without knowing the real reason. And here's the important part — this isn't just something that happens to patients with a cut brain wire. It happens in ordinary people, every day, including you and me. A lot of "why I did that" explanations we give are just our best guess, dressed up to sound like a fact.

Idea 5

Sometimes the two halves fight each other

Real life: Some patients pick a shirt off the rack with one hand — then their other hand puts it straight back. Or one hand picks up a ringing phone, and the other hand won't let it near their ear.

Doctors call this alien hand syndrome. One half of the brain seems to want something different from the other half, and they end up working against each other. It's a dramatic version of something small and normal — like wanting to eat the cake, and also wanting to say no to it, at the same time.

The Lesson

You are not just one voice in charge. You're made of several parts, and they don't always agree. The part of you that talks and explains "why" is not always the part that actually decided. So next time you're 100% sure why you did something — hold that answer a little more loosely.

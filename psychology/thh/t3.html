The Divided Self · Chapter 1

New vs. Old

Your brain didn't get replaced as it evolved. A newer, smarter layer just grew on top of a much older one — and the old one never left.

Idea 1

Your brain grew the way an old house grows

Real life: Picture a 100-year-old house. A porch got enclosed. Then it became a kitchen. Then a bedroom was added above it. Nothing was torn down — rooms just kept getting added on.

Brains evolved the same way. The very first, simplest brains had just three basic parts. Over millions of years, animals kept adding new parts on top of the old ones — instead of replacing them. Nothing gets thrown away. The old parts are still in there, doing their old job.

Idea 2

The old part: your instinct engine

Real life: Scientists sent a tiny electric current into one small spot in a rat's brain — and the rat instantly became aggressive. A different spot, and it became endlessly hungry.

That spot is part of what's called the limbic system — a set of old brain parts you share with most other mammals. It handles hunger, fear, memory, and basic emotion. It's not "dumb," it's just old, fast, and automatic.

Idea 3

The new part: your thinking cap

Real life: A cat reacts to a mouse the instant it sees one. A person can look at the same mouse and think, "should I set a trap, or just leave the window open?"

That extra pause — the ability to think it over instead of just reacting — comes from a newer outer layer called the neocortex, especially the part at the very front of your brain. This part isn't wired for one specific job. It's built to plan, weigh options, and think ahead.

Idea 4

A tidy story — that turns out to be wrong

Real life: A schoolteacher, previously a normal family man, suddenly started acting on urges he'd always been able to control. Doctors found a large tumor pressing on the front part of his brain. When they removed it, the behavior stopped. When the tumor grew back, it returned — and stopped again once it was removed a second time.

It's tempting to tell a neat story: the old brain is the beast, and the new front-brain is the tamer that keeps it in check. This case seems to prove it — remove the front brain's power, and the "beast" runs loose.

Not quite. That story assumes reason lives in the new front-brain, and emotion stays trapped in the old core. But scientists later found something surprising: a big chunk of that same front-brain isn't a cold "logic center" at all — it generates feelings. Keep reading.

Idea 5

The "thinking" brain can also feel

Real life: Some patients had a small part of their front brain damaged — not the "brakes," but a part just behind the eyes. Afterward, they said they felt almost nothing: no fear at frightening images, no warmth at happy ones. But they still scored perfectly on logic and intelligence tests.

Guess what happened when these patients, free of "distracting" emotions, went out into the world?

You'd expect them to become perfectly logical. The opposite happened. They struggled to make even simple choices — what to eat, what to wear, what to do next. Faced with a decision, they could list every pro and con, but nothing ever felt like the right answer, so they got stuck.

Turns out that gut feeling — the pull toward one option — is not the enemy of good decisions. It's what makes deciding possible at all.

The Lesson

Reason didn't replace emotion — it grew up alongside it, and depends on it. Picture a rider on an elephant: the newer brain gave you the rider, but it made the elephant smarter too. Good thinking isn't about silencing your gut. It's about the two working together.

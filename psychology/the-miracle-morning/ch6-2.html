1. V = Visualization

What is visualization?

Visualization—also called creative visualization or mental rehearsal—means creating mental pictures of specific behaviors and outcomes you want in your life.

In simple words: You mentally see yourself achieving something before you actually achieve it.

It isn't just imagining the final result. The author describes imagining:

  • What you want to achieve
  • How you will achieve it
  • The actions you need to take
  • Yourself performing those actions successfully

Visualization is frequently used by athletes to improve performance.

Famous examples

The book gives several examples of successful people who have advocated visualization: Bill Gates, Arnold Schwarzenegger, Anthony Robbins, Tiger Woods, Will Smith, Jim Carrey, Oprah.

Tiger Woods

Tiger Woods is described as using visualization to mentally rehearse the perfect execution of his golf swing.

Another famous golfer, Jack Nicklaus, said he never hit a shot without first having a sharp mental picture of it.

"For acting services rendered."

— Jim Carrey, memo line on a $10 million check he wrote himself in 1987, dated Thanksgiving 1995. In 1994 he was paid $10 million for Dumb and Dumber.

The important lesson isn't that writing a check magically creates money.

The useful idea is: Create a clear target → repeatedly imagine it → keep it psychologically present → take action toward it.

Why visualization?

The author says many people are trapped by their past. They repeatedly replay: previous failures, disappointments, heartbreaks, mistakes.

Instead, visualization allows you to deliberately create a picture of the future you want.

The purpose is to make your future vision stronger than your memories of past failures.

The author's 3-step visualization method

The author normally spends around 5 minutes visualizing. After reading affirmations, he sits upright, closes his eyes, takes slow, deep breaths, visualizes his ideal day, and imagines performing tasks with ease, confidence, and enjoyment.

When writing his book, he imagined himself writing easily and enjoying the creative process without stress, fear, or writer's block. He also visualized the finished book being read and enjoyed by people.

01

Get ready

The book recommends: sit comfortably, sit upright, breathe deeply, close your eyes, clear your mind.

You can sit on a chair, couch, or floor. Some people may also use quiet instrumental music, such as classical or baroque music.

02

Visualize what you really want

This is where you imagine your ideal future. The author asks you to temporarily forget logic, limits, practicality — and ask: What would I have, do, or become if I could choose freely?

Then visualize your major goals, deepest desires, life-changing dreams — involving your senses: see it, hear it, feel/touch it, smell it, taste it. The book argues that making the visualization more vivid can make you feel more compelled to take the actions required.

Example

Suppose your goal is becoming a software engineer. Don't simply imagine: "I'm a successful software engineer." Instead imagine: yourself studying programming, writing code, solving difficult problems, building projects, applying for opportunities, attending interviews, getting better at communication, eventually working as the professional you want to become. The process matters.

03

Visualize who you need to become

This is one of the most important ideas. Don't only visualize: What do I want? Also visualize: Who do I need to become to achieve it?

The author gives examples such as visualizing yourself: exercising, studying, working, writing, making calls, sending emails, taking action, making progress. And importantly, visualize yourself enjoying the process, rather than only imagining the reward.

So: Goal → person required → daily actions.

Visualization does NOT replace action

This is extremely important. The book explicitly warns about Vision Boards:

"Nothing changes in your life without action."

A vision board can increase motivation and help you focus, but taking the necessary actions is what produces results.

So: Visualization + Action > Visualization alone.

2. E = Exercise

Exercise

The second practice is Exercise. The author argues that morning exercise can: increase energy, improve health, improve self-confidence, improve emotional well-being, improve thinking, help concentration.

And even a few minutes can be useful as part of the morning routine.

Eben Pagan's advice

The book describes an interview involving personal-development expert Eben Pagan and Anthony Robbins. When asked his number-one key to success, Pagan answered: Start every morning with a personal success ritual.

He emphasized morning exercise — getting the heart rate up, and getting blood flowing and oxygen into the lungs. He specifically suggested 10–20 minutes of jumping jacks or some form of aerobic exercise in the morning.

Yoga

The author says that if he could choose only one type of exercise for the rest of his life, he would choose yoga. He describes yoga as combining stretching, strength training, cardio, focused breathing, potentially meditation.

The book also contains a contribution from yoga instructor Dashama, who describes yoga as having physical, mental, emotional, and spiritual applications.

The practical idea

You don't have to copy the author's preferred exercise. It can be walking, running, yoga, or another suitable workout. The important idea is to make movement part of the morning routine.

Why morning exercise?

The author identifies two common excuses: "I don't have time" and "I'm too tired." His solution is to exercise before the day has a chance to wear you down — removing many opportunities to postpone it.

Consult a doctor or physician before starting an exercise regimen, particularly if you have physical pain, discomfort, disabilities, etc.

— Safety note from the book
3. R = Reading

Reading

Reading is the fifth practice in S.A.V.E.R.S. The author calls reading a fast way to acquire: knowledge, ideas, strategies, skills.

The basic principle is: Learn from people who have already achieved what you want to achieve. Instead of reinventing everything yourself, study people who have already done it.

What should you read?

For financial success

Think and Grow Rich — Napoleon Hill
Secrets of the Millionaire Mind — T. Harv Eker
Total Money Makeover — Dave Ramsey

For relationships

The Five Love Languages — Gary D. Chapman
The SoulMate Experience — Jo Dunn
The Seven Principles for Making a Marriage Work — John M. Gottman and Nan Silver

The author's broader point is that books exist on almost every area of life you want to improve.

The 10-page rule

This is one of the most useful statistics in this section. The author recommends 10 pages per day. If you're new to reading or read slowly, he says 5 pages is okay to start.

Read with a purpose

Before reading, ask: Why am I reading this? What do you want to learn? The author also emphasizes something more important: Don't merely read. Implement what you learn. Knowledge that isn't applied doesn't create much change.

Re-reading is important

The author strongly recommends rereading valuable books. Why? Because mastery requires: Exposure → Practice → Repetition → Internalization.

He compares this with karate. You don't learn a technique once and automatically master it — you practice it repeatedly. Similarly, rereading a valuable book can be more useful than constantly jumping to new books before you've applied the lessons from the previous one.

He says he read Think and Grow Rich as many as three times and referred back to it throughout the year.

4. S = Scribing

Scribing

"Scribing" is basically the author's unusual word for: Writing. He needed an S to complete S.A.V.E.R.S., so he used "Scribing."

His favorite form is journaling — around 5–10 minutes. The idea is simple: Thoughts in your head → words on paper. Writing can help reveal ideas and insights that aren't obvious when they're only in your mind.

Why journal?

The author says journaling can help you document: insights, ideas, breakthroughs, realizations, successes, lessons, opportunities, personal growth, areas for improvement.

The author's first yearly journal review

After his first year of Miracle Morning journaling, he began reading his journal from the beginning on December 31, reviewing the year day by day. This allowed him to see how his mindset changed, what he had accomplished, what actions he had taken, how much he had grown, and lessons he had previously forgotten.

Gratitude 2.0

During this review, the author describes what he calls a "Back to the Future" moment — his current self looked back at his previous self's gratitude. So he experienced gratitude twice: past gratitude ("I was grateful for this experience then") and present gratitude ("I'm grateful now for how far I've come since then").

Accelerated growth

The author then created two columns — Lessons Learned and New Commitments: What did I learn? What will I do because of it? While reviewing hundreds of journal entries, he recovered dozens of valuable lessons and converted them into new commitments. He says this process contributed greatly to his personal growth.

Three additional benefits of journaling

  • Gain clarity — writing forces you to think through something enough to understand it; it can help with clarity, brainstorming, solving problems.
  • Capture ideas — writing prevents valuable ideas from disappearing.
  • Review lessons — you can return to what you learned.
  • Acknowledge progress — you can compare yourself with your previous self and see how far you've progressed.

Gap-focus

This is a particularly important psychological concept in this section. The author calls it Gap-focus: focusing on the distance between where I am and where I want to be — "I'm not good enough yet," "I haven't achieved enough," "I'm behind," "I should be better." The author says constant negative gap-focus can damage confidence and self-image.

But here's the interesting part... Gap-focus isn't always bad. The author says it can actually drive high achievers, because wanting to close the gap can motivate people to improve. The problem occurs when the gap becomes "I'm not enough" rather than "I'm excited and committed to reaching my potential."

"I'm doing the best I can right now" — and — "I can and will do better."

The balanced mindset — avoids feelings of inadequacy while maintaining ambition.

The 10-item example

Suppose you have 10 tasks on your to-do list. You complete 6/10. Negative gap-focus makes you think "I failed to finish everything." But the author asks you to notice: you successfully completed 6 things. He argues that people often remember the few things they did wrong while overlooking the many things they did right. Journaling can redirect attention toward what you accomplished, what you're grateful for, and what you'll improve tomorrow.

01

Choose your format

Physical notebook, digital journal, or journaling app. The author says the choice depends on personal preference: write by hand or type?

02

Get a journal

Even a cheap notebook works — the author mentions a 99-cent spiral notebook as an example. But he personally recommends a durable, dated journal with space for all 365 days: if you miss a day, the blank space makes you notice it and can motivate you to catch up.

03

Decide what to write

There are countless possibilities — gratitude, dreams, food, workouts, goals, plans, family, commitments, lessons, your day. The author's own method can include: What am I grateful for? What did I accomplish? What should I improve? What specific action will I take? He also sometimes simply writes a dated summary of his day.

USA Today survey — but the #1 obstacle identified in the passage is that they can't find the time. The author says he was writing the book at 6:03 a.m. during his Miracle Morning.
Customizing Life S.A.V.E.R.S.

Your Miracle Morning should fit your life

The author emphasizes that you don't have to copy one rigid routine. Your Miracle Morning can be 20 minutes, 30 minutes, 60 minutes, longer on weekends. You can also change the order.

For example, some people prefer exercise first — because it wakes them up. The author personally prefers silence → other practices → exercise last — because he can then shower and start the day. The important point: Your Miracle Morning should fit your life.

The deeper message of S.A.V.E.R.S.

The author says new experiences are difficult and uncomfortable before they become natural. He gives himself as an example: his first meditation experience was difficult because his mind was racing, and his first yoga experience was uncomfortable because he wasn't flexible and couldn't perform the poses properly. But with practice, both became natural to him.

So don't expect Day 1 → perfect routine. Instead: Practice → repetition → familiarity → habit.

The 6-Minute Miracle Morning

"I don't have time."

The author recognizes that the biggest objection people have is "I don't have time." So he created a compressed version called The 6-Minute Miracle Morning — designed for extremely busy days, people under pressure, people who feel overwhelmed, and days when adding another routine feels stressful. The author argues that spending at least 6 minutes developing yourself is reasonable even when you're extremely busy.

Each of the six S.A.V.E.R.S. gets approximately 1 minute.

01

Silence

Sit quietly. Breathe slowly and deeply. You can meditate, pray, practice gratitude, or simply be present. The purpose described is to calm your mind and body and create peace, purpose, and direction.

02

Affirmations

Read your affirmations aloud. They remind you of your potential, priorities, commitments, purpose, goals. The author says this can increase internal motivation and confidence and remind you what actions you need to take.

03

Visualization

Close your eyes or look at your vision board. Visualize reaching your goals, your day going well, enjoying your work, relationships, accomplishing what you intend to accomplish — experiencing what your desired future would look and feel like.

04

Scribing

Write down things you're grateful for, things you're proud of, results you're committed to creating that day. The purpose is to enter the day feeling more empowered, inspired, confident.

05

Reading

Read a page or two from a useful book. The idea is to obtain one new idea that you can potentially use that day — it might improve work, relationships, thinking, emotional state, life generally.

06

Exercise

Move for 60 seconds — running in place, jumping jacks, push-ups, sit-ups. The goal described is to raise your heart rate and increase alertness and focus.

The whole system in one picture

S — Silence → Calm your mind. A — Affirmations → Direct your thoughts. V — Visualization → Create a picture of where you're going. E — Exercise → Activate your body. R — Reading → Feed your mind with knowledge. S — Scribing → Capture your thoughts and lessons.

Then: Action during the day → Progress toward your goals.

The most important lesson

The book isn't ultimately saying: "Wake up early and everything will magically become better."

The deeper system is: Morning routine → personal development → clearer thinking → stronger intention → better actions → gradual improvement.

And one of the most important warnings in the chapter is: Visualization without action isn't enough. The vision gives you direction. The routine prepares you. But your actions create the actual results.

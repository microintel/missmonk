Chapter Notes · The Miracle Morning

It’s not the hour.
It’s the intention.

A framework you redesign around your own life, goals, schedule, and energy — not a rigid 5 AM ritual you force yourself into.

Scroll to read the chapter

Absolutely. This chapter is basically saying: The Miracle Morning is not a rigid “wake up at 5 AM and do exactly six things” system. It is a framework that you can redesign around your life, goals, schedule, and energy.

01

The central idea: customize it

The author says the Miracle Morning is 100% customizable.

You can change:

  • When you wake up
  • When you start your Miracle Morning
  • How long it lasts
  • Which Life S.A.V.E.R.S. activities you do
  • How much time you spend on each activity
  • The order of the activities
  • What you focus on
  • How you adapt it on weekends
  • How you adapt it when travelling or busy

The goal isn't to create a perfect morning routine. The goal is to create dedicated time for personal development so you can improve yourself and move toward your important goals.

02

You don't necessarily have to do it in the morning

This is one of the most important ideas in the chapter.

The author says: You don't actually have to do The Miracle Morning in the morning.

That sounds contradictory, but the reasoning is simple. Someone who works during the night has a completely different schedule from someone who sleeps at 9 PM. For example:

Person A

Sleeps: 9:30 PM  ·  Wakes: 5:30 AM

Miracle Morning: 5:30–6:30 AM

Person B — night-shift worker

Sleeps: 8:00 AM  ·  Wakes: 3:00 PM

Miracle Morning: 3:00–4:00 PM

Both can use the same principle.

The key principle

Wake up earlier than you normally would — the author typically suggests 30–60 minutes earlier — and use that time for intentional self-development.

So the important thing isn't:

“5 AM = success.”

It's:

“I deliberately create time for myself before the demands of the day take over.”
03

When should you eat?

The author spends considerable time discussing food because eating can affect how you feel during your Miracle Morning.

His recommendation is: Ideally, eat AFTER the Miracle Morning.

His reasoning is that digestion requires energy. A large meal can make you feel less alert and more sluggish. Therefore, he argues that doing your S.A.V.E.R.S. before breakfast may help you stay more alert and focused.

But what if you're hungry?

He doesn't say you must completely avoid food. If you need something before starting, he recommends keeping it small, light, and easy to digest, giving examples such as:

  • Fresh fruit
  • A smoothie

The important distinction is:

Don't turn your Miracle Morning into a period of discomfort just because you're following a rule. Adapt it to your body and schedule.

04

Why you eat matters

The author then makes a bigger argument about food. He says people frequently choose food primarily because:

  • It tastes good
  • They enjoy it
  • They're emotionally attached to it
  • They're craving it
  • It's convenient

For example, if someone asks:

“Why did you drink that soda?”

A common answer might be:

“Because I like it.”

The author argues that we should also consider another question:

“What will this food do for me?”

Think about:

  • Energy
  • Health
  • Mental focus
  • Long-term wellbeing
  • How you'll feel afterward

His point isn't:

“Only eat food that doesn't taste good.”

Quite the opposite. He argues that you can seek foods that are both enjoyable and supportive of health and sustained energy.

05

What should you drink?

The author refers back to his earlier 5-Step Snooze-Proof Wake Up Strategy. Step #4 was:

Drink a full glass of water.

The reasoning is that you've gone through the night without drinking, so water can help you rehydrate after sleep.

The author also says he typically starts his Miracle Morning by making a cup of tea. He even says he sets his alarm 15 minutes earlier specifically to give himself time to prepare the tea without taking time away from his Miracle Morning.

If someone prefers coffee, he says they can have coffee instead.

So again, the underlying message is:

Customize the routine rather than blindly copying someone else's routine.

06

What about food quality?

The author strongly emphasizes fruits and vegetables and what he calls “living foods.”

He claims that a diet rich in fresh fruits and vegetables can:

  • Increase energy
  • Improve mental focus
  • Support emotional wellbeing
  • Support health
  • Help protect against disease

He then introduces his own Miracle Morning Super-food Smoothie. According to the passage, he describes it as containing:

  • Complete protein
  • Essential amino acids
  • Antioxidants
  • Omega-3 essential fatty acids
  • Vitamins
  • Minerals
  • Cacao
  • Maca
  • Chia seeds

He associates these ingredients with things such as immunity, cardiovascular health, brain function, energy, mood, and other benefits.

Important distinction

These nutritional claims are the author's claims in the book, not statistics presented in this chapter. The passage doesn't provide numerical research data proving each claim. So don't interpret statements like “greatly increase your energy levels” as a specific scientific statistic.

07

Connect your Miracle Morning to your biggest goals

This is arguably the most useful section.

The author says many people use the Miracle Morning specifically to make progress toward goals they've been delaying. Examples include:

  • Starting a business
  • Writing a book
  • Building something important
  • Developing yourself
  • Pursuing a major dream

The S.A.V.E.R.S. become tools for keeping those goals visible.

Example

Suppose your goal is:

“I want to become a software developer.”

You could customize S.A.V.E.R.S. around that goal.

  • Silence → Clear your mind.
  • Affirmations → Remind yourself what you're working toward and what actions you need to take.
  • Visualization → Imagine yourself successfully completing projects, solving problems, and becoming capable.
  • Exercise → Get physically active and energized.
  • Reading → Learn programming concepts.
  • Scribing → Write down what you learned, what you're building, and what you'll do today.

Now the Miracle Morning isn't just:

“A morning routine.”

It's becoming:

“A daily system for moving toward your desired future.”
08

Affirmations should be connected to real action

The author says your affirmations shouldn't just contain vague statements. They should be aligned with:

  • Your goals
  • Your dreams
  • What you need to believe
  • What you need to think
  • What you need to do

So instead of merely saying:

“I will be successful.”

The affirmation should reinforce the behaviors and commitments required to become successful.

Why?

Because repeatedly reading your affirmations keeps your important priorities mentally visible. The intended chain is:

Affirmation → focus → commitment → action → progress
09

Visualization should include the process, not just the result

The author gives another important distinction. Don't only visualize:

“I achieved my dream.”

Visualize yourself going through the process of achieving it. For example, if you're writing a book:

Don't only imagine holding the finished book. Imagine yourself:

  • Sitting down to write
  • Continuing when it's difficult
  • Completing chapters
  • Reviewing your work
  • Eventually finishing the project

He also recommends making visualization vivid by involving your senses:

  • See
  • Feel
  • Taste
  • Touch
  • Smell

The idea is that a vivid mental picture can increase desire and motivation to take action.

10

Procrastination: “Do the worst first”

This is one of the chapter's strongest productivity ideas.

The author recommends starting your day with your most important or least enjoyable task.

This connects to Brian Tracy's idea from Eat That Frog. The metaphor is: if you have to eat a frog, you'd better do it first.

In practical terms, suppose you have three tasks:

  1. Check social media
  2. Watch tutorials
  3. Complete the difficult project you've been avoiding

Your brain naturally wants #1 and #2 because they're easier. But the author recommends: Do #3 first.

Why?

Because completing a difficult task can create a feeling of accomplishment and momentum, making the rest of the day easier to manage. The principle is:

Hard task → completion → psychological momentum → greater productivity
11

The Miracle Morning isn't really about the six activities

This is a subtle but extremely important point.

The author says the Miracle Morning is more about waking up with purpose than following a specific list of activities.

The purpose is to combine:

Intentional time + personal development + proactive action

The activities you choose should:

  • Be proactive.
  • Improve your inner world — your mind, emotions, beliefs, skills.
  • Improve your outer world — your actions, work, relationships, life.

Therefore, if your circumstances require modifications, that's okay.

12

What about weekends?

The author originally practiced the Miracle Morning Monday → Friday and took weekends off. But he noticed something.

On days when he practiced it, he generally felt:

  • Better
  • More fulfilled
  • More productive

On days when he slept in, he says he felt:

  • Lethargic
  • Unfocused
  • Unproductive

So he suggests experimenting for yourself. Try:

Monday–Friday: Miracle Morning

Saturday–Sunday: Sleep in

Then compare how you actually feel.

He also quotes Oprah Winfrey describing the advantage she found in waking early on Saturday: having quiet time to plan and work without weekday time pressure.

The important thing is personal observation, not blindly copying Oprah.

13

Don't let the routine become boring

Another major principle: your Miracle Morning should evolve.

The author says he has continued using the S.A.V.E.R.S., but changes elements of his routine to keep things interesting. For example:

Exercise

Change your exercise routine: monthly, every few months, when your circumstances change.

Meditation

Try different approaches.

Vision board

Create one and update it regularly.

Affirmations

Update them as your goals and vision change.

This matters because your life changes, so your routine should change too.

14

Your routine should change with your current project

The author gives several examples.

Preparing for a speech

If he has an upcoming speech or seminar, he allocates more Miracle Morning time toward practicing and rehearsing the presentation.

Travelling

If he's travelling, he adjusts his routine according to his environment.

Late-night event

If he has a late-night keynote or workshop, he moves his wake-up time later.

Writing the book

Ironically, while writing the very book you're reading, he says his Miracle Morning became heavily focused on completing the book. But he didn't completely abandon S.A.V.E.R.S. Instead: he shortened S.A.V.E.R.S. → created more time for writing.

That's a perfect example of customization.

15

Humans need variety

The author ends with a broader psychological lesson: repetition can become boring. And when something becomes boring, motivation can decrease.

He compares this to relationships and work: if something becomes stale, you have some responsibility for making it interesting again.

He recalls advice from an early mentor:

“Whose fault is it that it's boring? And whose responsibility is it to make it fun again?”

The lesson is:

Don't just complain about a boring routine. Change the routine.

16

The deeper message of the entire chapter

The chapter can be reduced to this:

Don't serve the routine. Make the routine serve you.

Your Miracle Morning should fit:

Your schedule ↓ Your lifestyle ↓ Your energy ↓ Your current priorities ↓ Your biggest goals ↓ Your changing circumstances

That's why there is no universally perfect Miracle Morning. Someone else's:

5:00 AM + 60 minutes + meditation + exercise + reading

doesn't automatically become your perfect routine. Your version might be:

7:00 AM + 20 minutes + reading + planning + exercise

or even a shortened version when you're extremely busy. The system is flexible.

17

The chapter's practical framework

Think of it like this:

YOUR LIFE ↓ Your schedule ↓ Your priorities ↓ Your biggest goal ↓ ┌────────────────────────┐ │ CUSTOMIZE S.A.V.E.R.S│ └────────────────────────┘ ↓ ↓ ↓ ↓ Mind Body Learning Action ↓ Daily progress ↓ Long-term change

And there are five major customization rules:

  1. Customize the time — You don't have to wake at a specific “magic” hour.
  2. Customize the duration — You don't need exactly one hour.
  3. Customize the activities — S.A.V.E.R.S. can be shortened or adapted.
  4. Customize the content — Make your affirmations, visualization, reading, and writing relevant to your actual goals.
  5. Customize it as life changes — College, work, travel, projects, weekends, deadlines — all can require adjustments.
18

One important correction about the “statistics”

This chapter contains very little numerical statistical data. The notable numbers/details actually given are:

100%customizable — the author's description of the system
30–60 minearlier — his typical suggested earlier wake-up window
15 minearlier — his example of setting his alarm to prepare tea
Monthlyor 90 days — how often exercise routines might be changed
Mon–Frihis original weekday-only practice, before experimenting with weekends
168 hrsper week — mentioned in Katie Heaney's opening testimonial

The chapter does not provide detailed sample sizes, percentages, controlled-study results, or statistical evidence for most of its health/productivity claims. So when explaining this chapter, it's important not to turn the author's personal experiences and recommendations into scientific statistics.

19

The simplest way to understand it

Imagine you have a huge goal. You don't have enough time during your normal day because college/work/messages/social media/family responsibilities keep taking your attention.

The Miracle Morning says:

Create a protected block of time before your normal responsibilities consume your attention.

Then use that time to:

Improve your mind → improve your body → learn → plan → work toward your goals

And when life changes:

Change the routine.

Not:

“I failed because I couldn't follow the perfect routine.”

Instead:

“My life changed, so I'll redesign my routine.”

That's the real message of this chapter.

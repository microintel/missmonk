Why Value a Company at All?Introduction

🤷 The Problem: Price ≠ Worth

It's difficult to tell whether a stock is overvalued or undervalued just by looking at its market price. That's why valuing the company itself — before you buy — matters. We must acquire stocks when they're trading below their intrinsic value.

💡 Relative valuation, which you met earlier, compares a company to similar companies without calculating its exact worth. Absolute valuation takes the opposite approach — it tries to identify the company's true value directly, by calculating an exact number for what the business is worth.

“The intrinsic value of a company is the discounted value of the cash that can be taken out of a business during its remaining life.” — Warren Buffett
3.1
DCF IntroductionDiscounted Cash Flow

📊 What is DCF?

DCF is a valuation method which estimates the current value of future cash flows. It takes two stages: first you forecast what the company will earn this year, next year, and years further out — only trustworthy if you genuinely understand the business. Then you discount those future profits back to today's value, because money loses value over time.

🧮 Adding up every discounted cash flow

You calculate a company's value by adding all discounted cash flows. Since the business is expected to survive, you get the present value of all cash flows generated by discounting them perpetually — the business is assumed to continue for a longer period of time.

V₀ = [ CF₁ + CF₂ + CF₃ ] + CF₄(Cost of Capital − Growth) where CF₁ = PAT ÷ (1 + r)ⁿ  ·  PAT = profit after tax  ·  r = discount rate  ·  n = years out

3.3
Problems with DCFWhy we go easy on this method

Problem with DCF

Three reasons retail investors tread carefully
🤔Too many assumptionsForecasting revenue, expenses and profit years out — plus picking a discount rate and growth rate — means every input is a guess about a business you don't run.
⚖️Too much weight on perpetual valueIt varies significantly with even a tiny change in the denominator. Over-reliance on this one number is a major warning sign — you just saw it swing with a one-point slider change.
📚Complicated for retail investorsFar more complicated than the median P/E chart and relative valuation — more research, more modelling, more judgment calls than most individuals can reliably make.
3.4
Relative or Absolute?Which one to choose

We've now seen the trouble with DCF and absolute valuation. And we already know relative valuation stops being useful when a company is losing money.

Relative

Compare to similar companies
  • More applicable — data is easily accessible for retail investors
  • Forecasting is unnecessary; fewer assumptions needed
  • Easier to begin with
  • Exit Multiple Approach is an excellent technique

Absolute

Calculate exact intrinsic value
  • Easier to identify the correct value of the company
  • More flexible to reflect your own assumptions

🏦 Institutional investors use both absolute and relative valuation — they have the time, effort and technical expertise for it, and employ valuation bands for relative work. As retail investors, we'll primarily use relative valuation via the Exit Multiple Approach. Feel free to play around with absolute valuation — just don't act on it yet.

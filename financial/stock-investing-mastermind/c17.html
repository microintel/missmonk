2.1 · Introduction
Why Selling Is
Just as Important
⚖️

Market fluctuations happen the moment we buy a stock — they're what lead to profits and losses when we eventually sell. So while buying the correct stock at the correct time is the key first step, it is equally important to sell it at the right time, at a profit. This chapter walks through five clear situations that should trigger a sell decision.

Reason 1 · Business Deterioration
We Book a Loss When
KPIs Deteriorate
📊📉🚚

A stagnant or decreasing KPI (Key Performance Indicator) is the very first sign that a business is deteriorating — and the impact shows up here before it ever reflects in the income statement or balance sheet.

For instance, when the number of trucks in a logistics firm goes flat and then starts declining, we should start asking questions:

Assume COVID-19, demonetization, or a GST announcement causes KPIs to stagnate for a while — that alone might not require action. But if KPIs deteriorate because of a fundamental shift, we can consider selling the stock, irrespective of profit or loss.

We may decide to sell our stocks due to fundamental shifts, a decline in KPIs, and deteriorating business conditions — travel companies take a long time to recover from heavy debt.

🔍 The rule: analyze whether it's a fundamental change or a small hiccup. If it's fundamental, sell the stock immediately — our investment thesis has gone wrong. It is very common for an investment thesis to go wrong; that's normal, not a failure.

Reason 1 (contd.) · Mindset
Why Taking a
Loss Is Good
😰➡️😌

What if you purchased a stock for ₹100, and because of the company's subpar performance, its value decreased to ₹70? We often keep holding, hoping the price recovers to ₹100 so we "don't take a loss" — but during that wait, it's entirely possible the price falls further, to ₹50.

📉 The Cost of Waiting

Holding a fundamentally broken stock, hoping to break even
Holding & hoping Sell at ₹70 & reinvest instead
🎯 The best option is to sell at ₹70 — book it as a loss, and invest that money in another stock which might rise to ₹200. Most retail investors avoid selling at a loss, but selling at a loss is actually a sign of a good investor. In this business you're likely to be wrong more often than right — so admit the mistake and move your money to a better place, rather than trying to recover it the same way you lost it.
Reason 2 · The Peter Lynch Principle
When Why You Bought
Is No Longer True
🌾🌻

"Cut your weeds
and water your flowers"

— PETER LYNCH

If the reason you bought a stock is no longer true — sell the stock immediately, irrespective of the price. Even if it's at a loss. We'll only make money on 30–40% of our investments, lose money on 30%, and just get our capital back on the remaining 30%. That's normal, even for the most sophisticated investors — so there's no harm in booking losses.

🌾

Cut the Weeds

Remove constant losersThey're weeds in your portfolio — companies constantly making losses.
Likely poor qualityPersistent losses usually signal a structurally weak business, not bad luck.
🌻

Water the Flowers

Keep the growersHold companies that are growing constantly — don't sell where you're making profit.
Let winners runLet winners become really big winners. That's how you grow wealth — don't cut your winners short.

As retail investors, we tend to make quick profits and sell shares immediately once the price rises — say from ₹100 to ₹250. That's cutting the flower rather than watering it — a huge mistake. Instead, we should wait: for any good company, a stock should climb from ₹250 to ₹500, and then to ₹1,000. We don't want to miss the entire upside by selling too quickly.

🌻 Patience vs. 🌾 Impatience

Two ₹100 stocks, two very different outcomes
The Flower — held with patience Sold too early at ₹250 The Weed — cut at ₹70
⏳ Due to a lack of patience, a lot of retail investors do the opposite of what they should: they cut their flowers and water their weeds. We need to do the reverse — sell loss-making companies once we're proved wrong, and never sell profit-making companies just because they've moved a little.
The Bigger Picture · Portfolio Math
What Should We Do
as Retail Investors?

Retail investors should let their winners become real big winners, and sell the loss-making companies — sell wherever the original hypothesis is no longer true.

Even the most sophisticated investors are often wrong; admit it, and move your money to a better place. Make sure your correct assumptions earn you 10–20x. If you're right just 5 out of 10 times, you'll become a great investor.
Reason 3 · Trust & Governance
Did Management Do
Something Wrong?
🚨🕵️‍♂️

We must immediately sell our stocks if there's critical news about the management:

①

The management admitting to a mistake.

②

Management implicated in some sort of fraud in the business.

③

Management not acting fairly toward the interests of shareholders.

Although selling such stocks may result in either profit or loss, we should still sell immediately if the management does something wrong. Two real instances were brought to light:

🏢Satyam
FINANCIAL DATA MANIPULATED

The management had been deceiving shareholders for a considerable amount of time by communicating incorrect financials. Any fraud by management — sell immediately. But it should be material; you shouldn't exit over every small thing.

🏦Yes Bank
GOVERNANCE BREAKDOWN

They might correct the issue later on, but it's too complicated for a retail investor to fully understand or track. It's best to sell at this point rather than wait and hope.

🤝 Trust breaks, and markets lose confidence in the management of a company. Any major misconduct against shareholders or the market has a significant effect on the enterprise — so sell the stocks immediately. Management can fix mistakes in the future, but tracking that recovery is far too complex for the average investor. It's best to sell now.
Reason 4 · Valuation Discipline
Too Expensive!
Outside the Valuation Band
💰📈🚩

We have countless companies and opportunities among listed stocks — we don't need to spend time on companies with a poor management track record, no matter how attractive the opportunity looks. When it comes to price, the rule is: sell when the valuation goes too far outside the valuation bands.

⚠️Problem 1: we may end up selling mid-way through a P/E re-rating.
⚠️Problem 2: we may sell on small movements and miss out on the long-term story.

If we expect the business to have long-term potential over the next 3–4 years, we should avoid selling — even if the valuation increases substantially. But if the market price becomes astronomically expensive and moves way outside the valuation range, we may end up selling. If the business has genuinely improved fundamentally, we might hold on even further.

📊 Stock A — The P/E Escalation

Trading at a P/E of 15–20 for 6–7 years, then suddenly escalating to 40–45
Price-to-Earnings ratio over time
🔎 Here, we try to determine what fundamental changes in the business led to the P/E re-rating. If we have solid reasons, we hold on. If we don't, we sell. In summary: don't sell on small movements or slight changes — you'll miss the long-term success. Do sell when the price escalates and the valuation goes way beyond your valuation bands.
Reason 5 · Mission Accomplished
When the Goal
Has Been Realized
🎯🏁

The most compelling reason to sell is when our goal is achieved. Once our hypothesis has played out and we've received the profits, we can either sell — because the story has played out — or participate in the next leg of the company's growth. We have two choices:

🌱

Retain the Stock

If we agree with the company's new goals and objectives, we may decide to retain the stock. If we want to be part of the company's future plans, we should update our investment thesis and hold on — then identify the correct KPIs to keep tracking.

🚪

Sell the Stock

If we believe the investment story has played out and there's limited room for growth, we can sell. For example, if the company introduces a new product very different from what it offered before, and it carries high risk, we may decide to sell.

🔁 If You Choose to Retain — Repeat the Process

1

Write down your assumptions again.

2

Write down the hypothesis and KPIs again.

3

Keep on tracking again.

4

Repeat the entire process.

The Bottom Line

Selling at a loss is not failure — holding a broken thesis out of hope is.
Selling a winner too early costs you far more than any single loss ever will.

Water your flowers, cut your weeds, and always sell when the reason you bought is no longer true.

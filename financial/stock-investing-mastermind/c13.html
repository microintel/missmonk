Introduction

Two families of valuation

The last chapter split valuation into two broad categories: absolute valuation (working out intrinsic worth from a company's own cash flows) and relative valuation — the subject of this page. Relative valuation uses ratios to compare a company's current price against a benchmark: its own history, its peers, or the wider market.

Every ratio below is read the same way: numerator is price or market value, denominator is a fundamental — something the business actually produces. A ratio can be compared three ways:

🏭
Across Industry

Comparing ratios of Company X, Y and Z within the same sector.

🕰️
Across Time

Comparing one company's own ratio across different financial years.

🌍
Across Geography

Comparing a company against similar businesses in other countries.

Valuation ratios for overall markets

Before judging one stock, it helps to know whether the whole market — the NIFTY, the Sensex — is running hot or cold. Two ratios answer that at the index level.

Reading the ratio

Ratio < 1 → the overall market is relatively undervalued (low priced). Ratio > 1 → the overall market is relatively overvalued (high priced). At 1.3, the market in this example is running ahead of the economy producing the earnings behind it.

2 · NIFTY P/E

Price-to-earnings tells you how many rupees an investor is paying today to buy one rupee of a company's annual earnings. Applied to the index, it tells you what multiple the whole market is trading at.

Formula
P/E  =  PriceEarnings   ·   EPS  =  Total EarningsTotal No. of Shares
Worked example — Reliance

Reliance earns ₹100 per share on average and trades at ₹2,200. P/E = 2200 ⁄ 100 = 22×. A person is paying ₹22 today for every ₹1 of Reliance's annual earnings.

Try it — Price / Earnings

P/E multiple
22×
22 rupees paid per ₹1 earned
Conclusion

Higher-quality earnings, with less risk and more predictability, are valued higher and given a higher multiple. But a higher multiple also means the stock is more expensive. A high multiple can mean either — expensive, or genuinely high quality. That distinction takes analysis, not just the number.

Section 2.2

Valuation ratios for individual stocks

"Should we buy HDFC Ltd. at current levels?" "Is Reliance overpriced?" — valuation ratios compare a stock's market value against its own earnings, assets, or fundamentals, so questions like these get a real answer instead of a guess.

Formula
P/E  =  Current PriceTTM Diluted EPS

TTM — "trailing twelve months" — is a company's financials for the last four reported quarters, whichever calendar year they fall in. It's always current, never stale by more than one quarter.

Conclusion

When P/E sits below its own median, it's a good time to buy — the stock is relatively cheaper and returns tend to be better. Above the median, it's relatively over-priced. One caveat: if EPS is negative for an extended period, the P/E ratio breaks down entirely and stops being useful for a buy or sell decision.

2 · Price / Book

Formula
P/B  =  Current PriceLast Disclosed Book Value

Every year a company earns a net profit. That profit goes one of two places: paid out as dividends, or retained and reinvested. Retained profit becomes Reserves on the balance sheet — money that belongs to shareholders and funds the business's day-to-day growth.

Book value
Book Value  =  Share Capital + Reserves & Surplus

Companies that generate an above-par return on equity, with high-quality earnings and low capital needs to grow, earn a premium and trade at a high Price/Book. The job is to work out whether that premium reflects real quality — or just an expensive stock.

3 · EV / EBITDA and EV / EBIT

Enterprise Value
EV  =  Market Cap + Debt − Cash − Financial Investments
EBITDA
EBITDA  =  Profit Before Tax + Depreciation + Interest

EV looks at the price of the whole business — equity plus debt, minus what's sitting in cash — rather than just the equity slice. EBITDA strips out interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation to leave pure operating earning power. EV/EBITDA is used especially where depreciation is heavy; EV/EBIT is the same idea but leaves depreciation and amortisation back in the denominator, so it suits businesses where those charges are light.

Section 2.4

Valuation bands

Ratios don't wander freely — they oscillate inside a range as market sentiment swings, then tend to return to it. That range is the valuation band: expensive on the high side, cheap on the low side. Bands are what keep an investor from overpaying when everyone else is euphoric.

Median P/E ≈ 27.4, band roughly 20×–32×

P/E rises on positive sentiment, falls on negative sentiment. Even through COVID-19's pessimism and the quick recovery after, earnings didn't swing nearly as violently as prices — so the P/E band held between roughly 20× and 32× across five years, COVID included.

Reading a band

A company is relatively expensive on the higher side of its band, and relatively cheap on the lower side. Bands keep you disciplined in a bull run and stop you from mistaking momentum for value.

When a valuation band stops working

01

The business changes fundamentally. When Reliance expanded from an oil-and-gas company into a consumer-facing, technology-oriented business (Jio), its old band became obsolete.

02

A P/E re-rating. The market re-prices the whole band because the underlying quality of the business has genuinely changed.

03

A major headwind or tailwind. Good news or bad news tied to the company shifts its real value, not just sentiment around it.

04

A major corporate event. Divestitures, acquisitions, and mergers can leave the old band irrelevant overnight.

1.1 · Phases of the Market Cycle

Markets breathe in four beats

Depending on valuation, sentiment and money flowing through the system, markets rotate through four repeating phases: Accumulation, Mark-Up, Distribution and Decline. Understanding which beat is playing tells you what to expect next.

PHASE 1

Accumulation

Operates around the bottom. Smart money starts buying. Valuation-driven market.

PHASE 2

Mark-Up

Prices increase. Different investors enter at different stages. Market turns bullish.

PHASE 4

Decline

Prices fall. Panic selling and fear grip security holders. Market turns bearish.

PHASE 3

Distribution

Around the top. Smart money sells while the public buys. Excitement-driven market.

🔁 As retail investors, we need to identify not just today's phase, but which phase comes next. Accumulation is always followed by Mark-Up. Distribution is always followed by Decline. In real markets there is no hard line between phases — so treat this as a rough map, not GPS.

📈 S&P 500 — a real 11-year cycle

1992 – 2003 · the same four phases you just saw, mapped onto a real index
Accumulation · 1992–94 Mark-Up · 1994–98 Distribution · 1998–2000 Decline / Markdown · 2000–03

Accumulation

This phase begins right near the bottom, after a significant fall, when the market is under-priced relative to its real worth. Institutional and professional "smart money" investors quietly start buying, because the market here is driven by low valuation rather than hype. High-quality stocks at attractive prices get bought; weak, low-quality stocks stay ignored.

Nomenclature tip: we name each phase after what smart money is doing — here, they're accumulating ownership.

Mark-Up

Prices start climbing. Because smart money already built positions during accumulation, retail participation now increases — a wide range of investors jump in at different stages, buying into different companies. As the rally strengthens, we call this a bull market. Confidence keeps building: fear that dominated the earlier decline fades, replaced by optimism.

Preceded by Accumulation → confidence rebuilds as fear declines.

Distribution

The market is now excitement-driven rather than valuation-driven. Ownership starts shifting from a small circle of institutions into the hands of the general public. Due to market euphoria, smart money quietly begins selling into the enthusiasm while retail investors keep buying — the ownership base "distributes" outward.

Earlier: valuation-driven. Now: excitement-driven.

Decline

As prices start falling, the earlier excitement fades fast. Widespread fear spreads among stockholders, triggering panic selling. By the time this phase runs its course, the market has firmly entered a bearish stage — which, in turn, sets up the conditions for the next Accumulation phase to begin. The cycle repeats.

Decline is always followed by a fresh Accumulation phase.
1.2 · Broad Factors that Affect the Cycle

Two forces drive market value

A company's real value comes from the quality and quantity of its earnings. But its market value — the price you see quoted — is largely driven by two separate forces: overall Sentiment, and Liquidity, i.e. the total inflow and outflow of capital.

Sentiment

The amount of confidence and optimism people have in the market. A positive long-term outlook translates directly into positive long-term sentiment; a negative outlook drags sentiment down with it.

Positive Outlook
→
Positive Sentiment
Negative Outlook
→
Negative Sentiment
EVENTS → POSITIVE SENTIMENT
  • Controlled inflation numbers
  • Growth in the Industrial Production Index
  • Growth in GDP
EVENTS → NEGATIVE SENTIMENT
  • Geopolitical wars & conflict
  • High inflation in the economy
  • Political instability in a country

Liquidity

Liquidity is the availability of funds or cash in the system. It's high when interest rates are low, the economy is healthy, and banks are lending freely — in short, when there's ample cash ready to enter the market.

💰 Availability of Capital

Cheap, abundant capital increases demand for stocks — and pushes market value up.

📉 Cost of Capital

The interest rate you must pay to borrow. Low interest rates = low cost of capital = companies can raise more money with less dilution.

Liquidity lets companies raise more money, at a cheaper cost, so more capital flows into the market.

🦠 March 2020 — COVID-19

Immediate: Widespread fear sent negative sentiment straight into prices — markets fell sharply.

A few months later: long-term fear around COVID subsided as people learned to manage it, and sentiment recovered.

⚔️ War Scare

When countries enter conflict, negative sentiment spreads through the market almost instantly — and stock prices fall in response, even before any direct economic damage is visible.

🧭 Reading the news against the cycle

Every headline you read is really about one of two things: is liquidity expanding or shrinking, and is confidence improving or fading?
Poor sentiment · limited liquidity Liquidity expanding · sentiment improving Sentiment positive · liquidity abundant Either falls, and the other follows
1.3 · Psychology of the Market

Every cycle is really an emotional cycle

Layer human emotion on top of the price chart and the four phases split into thirteen distinct feelings — from disbelief at the bottom, through euphoria at the top, and back down through panic and depression. Most retail investors buy near the top and sell near the bottom simply because they're following their feelings, not their valuation.

😨 → 🤑 → 😭 → 😐 — the full emotional wave

Scroll into view to watch the wave draw itself, exactly like price action feels in real time.

😔
Disbelief

People aren't confident yet — they're still nursing losses. It takes real time before confidence returns.

🙂
Hope

A slight improvement instills hope for a new beginning, but few have the courage to act on it yet.

😊
Optimism

Confidence is still fragile. Past losses hold people back, so buying starts only in small quantities.

😄
Belief

As the market keeps climbing, genuine confidence develops. Retail investors start buying new stocks — order returns to the market.

🥰
Thrill

Rising prices bring paper profits, and paper profits bring even more confidence and excitement.

🤑
Euphoria

The peak. Everyone is talking, predicting, and forecasting years of growth. No one wants to miss out.

😎
Complacency

A minor dip barely registers — people assume it's just a small hiccup and the market will keep climbing.

😟
Anxiety

A basic reality check kicks in. Smart money has quietly begun exiting; the public still holds on.

😑
Denial

People refuse to accept a significant decline is underway. They believe a recovery is just around the corner.

😰
Panic

As prices keep falling, retail investors experience real losses and rush to sell out of fear.

😫
Capitulation

Anger sets in. Investors are furious about ever having participated, and finally give up on their positions.

😞
Depression

The rapid fall turns into a slow grind down. People lose all confidence, log painful losses, and exit — often for good.

🔁 Disbelief eventually follows Depression, the market begins to rise again, and the entire cycle restarts. As disciplined investors, we should try to act on valuation, not on which emotion is currently loudest — because most retail money buys high (Euphoria) and sells low (Panic/Capitulation).
1.4 · Business Cycles

The economy has its own slower cycle

Business cycles are long-term — they run for years, not months — and they change a company's real value directly, by affecting the size, quality and quantity of its earnings. Everything hinges on one relationship: how much the market demands vs. how much industry actually supplies.

A good or bad industry position comes down to this comparison:

Demand LIMITED + most companies struggling → industry in trouble
Demand, optimism & earnings all rising → industry firing on all cylinders

Six stages make up one full business cycle: Expansion → Peak → Recession → Depression → Trough → Recovery — and then Expansion begins again.

📊 Demand > Supply
More demand than supply is available

People are willing to pay extra money to get the product → High Profits for whoever is supplying it.

📉 Demand < Supply
Higher supply means lower prices realised

Everyone tries to undercut everyone else to sell → Low Profits / Loss for the manufacturers.

🏭 Shah & Co. — a worked example

The market demands ₹1,000 Cr worth of hair oil every year. The whole industry currently supplies only ₹600 Cr worth — Shah & Co. alone makes 100 of those units. Because demand outstrips supply, Shah & Co. decides to expand.

Shah & Co. produces
100 units
Industry produces
600 units
Market demand
1000 units

Shah & Co. keeps investing to grow supply from 100 → 150 units, chasing the profit gap between demand and supply. As every player in the industry does the same, total supply keeps climbing — this is the Expansion phase in action. Eventually industry supply catches up to, and then overshoots, the ₹1,000 Cr demand — pushing the cycle toward its Peak.

1
Expansion

When supply can't keep up with existing demand, businesses expand. Both the business and its products are generally doing well, and companies keep investing to grow supply and keep up with rising demand.

2
Peak

Demand stabilises while supply, still chasing profits, keeps growing. Investment keeps flowing in — but demand has topped out, so supply is about to overtake it.

3
Recession

Supply is still growing, but total demand starts decreasing. Profits, which depend on that gap, begin to drop.

4
Depression

Supply now exceeds demand and profits turn very thin. Companies start incurring losses; weaker players exit the market entirely, unable to cover production costs. Supply finally starts to fall.

5
Trough

Demand and supply are out of sync. Demand keeps falling until it bottoms out; supply also declines, but more slowly, until it too reaches its lowest point — the trough is the floor of the entire cycle.

6
Recovery

Demand starts picking up again while supply, still shrinking, may even fall below it. With significant demand returning, industry players ramp up supply again — nudging the cycle back into Expansion.

4–5 yrs

Typical business-cycle length for faster-moving industries.

12–13 yrs

Typical length for slower, capital-heavy industries.

🔍

Tracking a business cycle accurately takes real effort, expertise and industry knowledge.

The Bottom Line

Market cycles run on sentiment and liquidity, and repeat in four beats: Accumulation, Mark-Up, Distribution, Decline.

Business cycles run on demand and supply, and repeat in six stages: Expansion, Peak, Recession, Depression, Trough, Recovery.

Both cycles are driven by the same underlying force — human emotion swinging between fear and greed. The disciplined investor learns to recognise the phase, not chase the feeling.

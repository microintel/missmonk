Introduction

In the previous section, we learned to evaluate companies — generating investment ideas, gathering qualitative information, and analyzing businesses to make decisions.

But once we've found a business worth owning, one question remains: does the price it's trading at make sense? Buy an excellent company at an exorbitant price, and you'll still suffer poor results. Finding the right price to buy — or, more importantly, sell — is just as important as finding the right stock.

What follows is Stock Valuation, taught from a retail investor's point of view. Institutional investors reach for far more sophisticated machinery — we won't need it.

1.1

What is Valuation?

Identifying quality companies that generate good profits at low cost is the first step. But choose a competent, well-managed company and pay an exorbitant price for it, and the result is the same: poor returns. You paid more than the stock's genuine value.

Market Value

The current price of a company's stock — the number quoted on CNBC or in apps like Money Control. It's whatever the crowd is willing to pay right now.

Intrinsic Value

The real value of the company, based on the quality and quantity of its earnings, assets, and liabilities. Something an analyst has to derive — it isn't quoted anywhere.

Valuation is an Opinion

Two people, two truths

Two analysts can look at the same company and arrive at entirely different valuations — because valuation rests on an assumption about the future, and nobody can know the future for certain. Try it: drag the growth assumption below and watch the "true value" move.

Science

Stock prices have a basic science to them, but pinning down intrinsic value takes real subjectivity — even professionals following the same method can land on different numbers. Valuation is the best guess of what a company is worth, used as groundwork to decide whether to buy or sell at today's price.

1.2

Two kinds of valuation

Relative Valuation
Ratios · comparison

Uses ratios and compares a company with its own past, or with direct competitors. The analyst doesn't try to pin an exact value on the asset — just checks whether they're comfortable buying at this relative price.

P/EP/BEV / EBITDA
Absolute Valuation
DCF · estimation

Estimates the current value of all the profits and cash a company will generate in the future. No comparison is done — the analyst derives one exact value, or a range.

Discounted Cash Flow

Where each method can mislead you

Relative Valuation

  • You might overlook the real reason a company is genuinely low-priced.
  • It may be pricing in a risk you don't yet understand.
  • Its quality of earnings may simply be lower than its competitors'.
  • Miss the reason, and you can end up buying an overvalued company while thinking it's cheap.

Absolute Valuation

  • The Rs 100 estimate is only as good as the assumptions behind it.
  • A predetermined bullish outlook can bias the whole model.
  • Biased assumptions produce a biased valuation — and a wrong entry price.

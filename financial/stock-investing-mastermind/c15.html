Section 4 · Valuation Framework

Exit Multiple
Approach

A simple way to estimate what you might actually make when you sell — built from just two moving parts: the future P/E and the future EPS.

Exit Price  =  Future EPS  ×  Future P/E
What It Solves
Working Backward
From The Exit

The Question Every Buyer Should Ask

Every other valuation method in this book looks at a company today. The exit multiple approach flips the question: if I buy this share now, hold it for a few years, and then sell it — what return could I realistically walk away with?

It doesn't need a discount rate, a terminal growth assumption, or a spreadsheet full of future cash flows. It only needs two honest guesses about the company's future — its earnings growth, and the multiple the market might place on those earnings — and it turns both into a single, current-price-anchored return.

The Process
Four Steps To
A Return Estimate

Each step narrows the guesswork a little further, until you're left with a range of numbers instead of a single wild guess.

1
⏱️

Assume a time horizon for exit

Pick the year you plan to sell — typically 3 to 5 years out. You are not trying to value the company forever, just up to the point you'll cash out.

2
⏳

Current P/E, and a range for future P/E

Note today's price-to-earnings ratio, then estimate a realistic band the market might award the stock at exit — multiples rise when confidence grows and shrink when it fades.

3
📈

Range of EPS growth

Based on your research of the business, estimate a believable low-to-high band for how fast earnings per share could grow each year until exit.

4
🧮

Calculate the likely return range

Combine every P/E guess with every growth guess against today's market price, to see the full spread of outcomes rather than a single number.

The Core Idea
Only Two Numbers
Do The Work

The method reduces the entire future of the stock to two levers: what multiple the market will pay for its earnings at exit, and how large those earnings will have grown by then.

Exit Price = Future EPS × Exit P/E
Future EPS = Current EPS × (1 + growth rate) raised to the number of years held

Why P/E, not cash flow?

A retail investor rarely has the tools to forecast free cash flow with precision. P/E is something you can observe on the stock's own history and on comparable companies — it's a shortcut that trades precision for practicality.

Why a range, not one number?

Nobody knows the exact multiple or exact growth rate three years out. By testing a spread of both, you replace a single fragile guess with a band of outcomes you can actually reason about.

Reading The Table
Low Numbers, High
Numbers — What They Mean
Cells trending low

If most of your realistic band of outcomes shows thin or negative returns, the share is already priced for a fairly generous future — there isn't much room left for you as a new buyer.

Cells trending high

If your realistic band shows comfortably positive returns across most combinations, the current price hasn't fully priced in the company's likely growth — that's the setup that makes a purchase appealing.

In the book's own example, the shaded band (exit P/E of 15–22, growth of 4–12%) produces returns from roughly −6% to +16% a year — a wide enough spread that the decision comes down to how confident you are in the low end versus the high end.

Before You Build The Table
Four Assumptions
You Must Set Honestly
01
Time horizon

Pick 3–5 years. Don't try to model returns into perpetuity — this method is deliberately short and finite.

02
EPS growth band

Ground it in the company's own history and its sector's growth, not hope. A wide but honest band (like 4–12%) is better than a falsely narrow one.

03
Exit P/E band

Look at the stock's own historical P/E range and where similar companies trade. A multiple that's never happened before is not a safe assumption.

04
Current market price

Every return in the table is measured against today's actual price — this is the one number you don't get to assume, only observe.

Running This Yourself
Four Inputs You
Can Look Up Today

Where each number comes from

  1. Current market price — pull it straight from the market; it's the only figure that isn't an estimate.
  2. Current EPS — read it off a stock screener or the company's latest annual report.
  3. Current P/E — simply current price divided by current EPS; you don't need to look it up separately.
  4. Time horizon — commit to 3–5 years and build your growth and P/E bands around that window.

The Bottom Line

The exit multiple approach closes out the valuation module by asking a forward-looking question instead of a present-day one: given a believable range of future EPS and future P/E, what could you actually earn?

It should be used with care — the entire output is only as good as the growth and multiple bands you feed into it. But grounded in real research rather than hope, it gives a retail investor a workable, honest range to make a buy decision against.

🧭 Introduction

So far we've covered idea generation, evaluation, and valuation — so we know what to buy. The critical remaining piece is knowing when to buy and at what price. Price is the only variable we control.

Once we purchase a stock: the market decides what happens to the price, and the management decides what happens to the company.

We are rewarded only when we team up with the right management, choose the right value, and the market supports the price we entered.
Section 1.1
What Price do we
Buy At?

Three ideas anchor every buying decision: patience to wait for the market's opportunity, discipline to buy good companies at attractive valuations, and a clear reason for every purchase.

⏳

Patience

Patience is very important. Wait for the market to give you the opportunity — even for companies you have fallen in love with.

🏢💰

Good Companies at Attractive Valuations

Buy good companies as per valuation bands. Combine this discipline with the Exit Multiple Approach.

❓

Reason

Know why you are buying. Every purchase needs a logical, written-down reason.

Patience will be tested at the core.

A company can look very good on analysis — but if it trades at a high price, and exit-multiple metrics show a negative expected return, it is a bad investment. A good company does not always imply a good investment. If it is truly a good company, we wait months, sometimes years, until the market offers us a chance, or the price falls into the lower quarter of the valuation band. This reduces the chance of overpaying on positive sentiment, protects our downside, and leaves room for upside. Paying a high price already hands most of the value to the market — leaving little upside for us.

📊 The Valuation Band — Where Should We Buy?

The lower quarter of a stock's historical valuation band is where patient buyers step in.

We analyze a company on industry, management, and financials to decide if it is good. Once we like the fundamentals, we still wait for an attractive valuation. Doing only one of the two ends in poor returns.

❓ Reason — Know Why You're Buying

Before buying any stock, put together your investment thesis by asking:

  • 🆕Is the product new to the market and expected to perform well?
  • 📊Does the company hold a large market share it plans to keep for the next five years?
  • 🏭Is the company developing a value-added product or a new manufacturing facility?
  • 🔁Even for a mature company — is it doing well now, and likely to continue?
There has to be a proper logical reason behind each of our investments.

🔭 Favourable Valuations & Long-Term Outlook

📦

Relevant in 10 Years?

Do we expect the company's product or service to still be relevant 5–7 years from now?

📈

Trends Benefit the Company

Current market changes and trends work in the company's favour.

🏃‍♂️🏃‍♀️

Edge Over Competitors

The company holds real advantages that rivals cannot easily copy.

🍪 Britannia — Product Relevance

Britannia's biscuits have stayed relevant for the last 100 years and are expected to remain so for the next 8–10 years. Britannia also runs nearly 4 million distributors reaching every town in India — a distribution moat that new entrants and copycats cannot match.

🚗 Petrol Cars — A Caution Flag

We stay skeptical that petrol cars remain relevant long-term as they face stiff competition from EVs. Since this is a riskier bet, extra caution is warranted here.

💳 Paytm & MobiKwik — Trend Tailwind
🅿️ Paytm Ⓜ️ MobiKwik

These digital-payment platforms benefit significantly from the ongoing shift toward digital transactions — giving them a favourable long-term outlook.

🍪 Brand Moat vs Copycats

Good companies always attract copycats trying to imitate their products for profit — similar-looking biscuits to Britannia or Parle-G exist, but without the brand recognition. Britannia and Parle-G will always hold the advantage.

If the answer to at least one of these questions is positive, the company has a positive long-term outlook. Combine this with favourable valuations, and you have a good chance of finding a winning company.

🔄 Invest When Something Is Changing

When a business changes fundamentally, its past valuations no longer apply.

🚀
New Product Launch

A new opportunity opens up — evaluate the product and market, then consider investing.

👔
Management Change

A poorly-run company under new leadership deserves a fresh look.

👨‍👩‍👧
Next Generation Entering

Fresh energy from the promoter family's next generation can bring new change.

🤝
Merger & Acquisition

Can create real synergies — but is risky, so evaluate carefully.

🌍
Entry into New Markets

An Indian company entering China, Japan, or the US may capture new share.

⚠️ Stay Selective

Understanding these changes takes time and skill. Retail investors must be extremely selective here and stick to what they truly understand. Keep investing as simple as possible.

Section 1.2
Investment
Philosophies

An investment philosophy is the set of beliefs and principles, built over time, that guides our buying decisions. It is shaped by education and market experience, differs from person to person, and is never formed overnight. Everyone makes mistakes — not only at the start, but later too. Not every investment will work out.

💡 Why It Matters

"It plays a key role in our decision of what to buy and when to buy." Below are the four most common investment philosophies.

💎

Value Investing

Finds a business trading below its estimated true value — companies that are cheap. Focus is on price paid, not growth. Investors want really favourable valuations.

Real Value > Market Value
🌱

Growth Investing

Seeks companies growing fast. Valuation can be high, but rapid growth is expected to justify the premium paid over time.

Real Value < Market Value
⚖️

GARP

Growth At a Reasonable Price. Balances growth and value — moderate EPS growth, at reasonable valuations. Some overpaying is fine, but never extraordinary.

Growth + Value Discipline
🏦

Income-Based Investing

Built around dividends, not capital gains — a favourite of retirees needing steady cash flow to cover living expenses. Emphasis is on dividends and cash flow, then reinvestment.

Dividends > Capital Gains

🧮 The Math Behind Each Philosophy

💎 Value Investing Example
Initial investment: ₹60
Market price rises: ₹100 → ₹120
Your gain is amplified because you entered at ₹60, not ₹100
Belief: stable business, true value > market value
🌱 Growth Investing Example
Buy over-priced stock: market value ₹100, pay ₹120
Belief: ₹100 of value grows to ₹200 in 2–3 years
Investors accept a premium today for tomorrow's growth
Risk: if growth doesn't play out, downside is real

The general thought process of a value investor leans lower on earnings growth and higher on P/E re-rating. For the growth investor, growth in EPS is the main priority.

💎 In the Case of Value Investing

Investors buy low P/E companies and expect to exit at a higher P/E, earning from P/E re-rating even if growth is low. Even if the thesis fails, they're protected on the downside since they never overpaid.

🌱 In the Case of Growth Investing

The investor is comfortable buying a high P/E stock if EPS growth is expected to be really high. Downside is significant too, since they are buying expensive.

📝 End Note

Every investor develops a philosophy that guides stock selection. A value investor waits patiently for attractive valuations. A growth investor may take a hit on price and move forward for growth — but risks paying significantly more, with a relatively higher downside if the growth story doesn't play out.

Section 1.4
At the Time of Buying —
Know Your Hypothesis
Know your reasons before buying any stock. Because if you don't know why you bought something, you will never know when to sell.

Before every investment, answer three questions — be specific, and use numbers or figures wherever possible.

1️⃣ "I am making this investment because…"
Be very specific — write down the facts you've verified from research.
  • The product of the company is a breakthrough.
  • 95% of market share is held by the company.
2️⃣ "I think I will make a good return because…"
Write down every assumption about the company that needs to hold true for good returns.
  • The company releases a new product that boosts sales and expands margins.
  • The company merged, and we anticipate a positive return from synergies.
3️⃣ "The risks that I know I am taking are…"
What risks are you aware of, and choosing to accept anyway?
  • EV poses a risk to petrol vehicles — via battery tech or safety concerns.
  • Asset-liability mismatch risk in banks — e.g. ₹100 crore short-term liability needs ₹100 crore of liquid assets to cover it.
🦠
Worked Example: COVID & Online Education

After COVID-19, we assume offline education drops 40% in customers, so online education benefits significantly. But once restrictions lift, people may prefer offline activities again — contradicting our assumption. This effect may play out one or two years after COVID. That's why we write down our thought process for future reference, and refer back to it every few months to check if something has changed.

⚠️ When Assumptions Break

If our assumptions turn out incorrect, our investment will give a sub-par result — and that is exactly how it should be. This is where we should sell, even if we lose money.
